## Methods: adjustment for race and ethnicity

All poverty models adjusted for child age, sex, data collection site and caregiver-reported race and ethnicity (ABCD's five-level variable: White, Black, Hispanic, Asian, Other; reference White). We include race and ethnicity as a marker of exposure to structural racism, which shapes both household income and children's mental health through discrimination, residential segregation and unequal access to services, and not as a biological characteristic of children. Without this adjustment, part of the association between income and symptom class could reflect these other pathways. Models adjusted only for age, sex and site are reported as a sensitivity analysis.

Adjustment increased rather than reduced the association. The median relative risk ratio across universes was 1.22 with adjustment and 1.16 without, and the adjusted estimate was higher in almost every universe (median ratio of adjusted to unadjusted 1.05, IQR 1.03-1.07, similar on every scale). This pattern suggests that, within this sample, groups with lower average income are not over-represented in the highest-symptom classes to the degree their income alone would predict, so that leaving race and ethnicity out of the model understates the income gradient. We do not interpret the race and ethnicity coefficients themselves.

## Discussion: caveat on the shape of the gradient

Our primary contrast compares the highest-symptom class with the lowest-symptom class. On raw scores this contrast summarises a gradient that rises steadily across classes in most universes (74%). On T-scores and log-transformed scores it does not: the lowest class is a group of children near the floor of the scale (median 21% of children with a mean raw CBCL total of 3.9 on T-scores; 12% with 2.2 on the log scale), and their families were on average poorer than those of children in mildly symptomatic middle classes. A middle class was significantly less likely to be poor than the lowest class in 56% of T-score and 75% of log-scale universes, more often at larger k. The highest-versus-lowest estimate is therefore not a simple dose-response on these scales, and its size depends on who ends up in the reference class. We report the full gradient (Supplementary Figures g1-g3) and do not interpret the U-shape substantively; it may reflect how floor effects and transformation define the lowest class rather than a protective effect of mild symptoms.
